Section 01

Colour — the formula before the palette

A palette isn't a handful of pretty hues. It's a small set of roles, each with a job. Get the roles right and almost any hue can become a coherent palette — which is exactly how the platform's existing twenty-strong Nuancier scales without each one being hand-tuned from nothing.

This skeleton is hue-less on purpose. Section 02 fills it in nine different ways — the roles never change, only the hue poured into them.

The lightness-step method

Rather than picking nine colours by eye, step one hue through consistent lightness increments. In dark mode: canvas around L8–10%, surface L14–16%, raised surface L20–22%, ink L92%+. In light mode, invert it: canvas L96–98%, surface white or L99%, ink L15–18%. Because every step is a fixed distance from its neighbour, contrast is correct by construction rather than by luck — and any future palette can be generated by picking one hue and running it through the same steps.

The one rule that overrides every palette below

Seven clinical severity levels are shown here compressed to four for space. Whichever hues a palette uses for decoration, these badges never participate — they are drawn from a locked, separate set of tokens that no Nuancier selection can touch. That's P0-12 from the audit, and every demo from here on proves it by leaving these untouched no matter what else changes on the page.

Section 03

Colour — combination rules

Nine good palettes can still produce a bad screen. These are the rules that keep any one of them disciplined once real content lands on top.

70 / 25 / 5, not 60 / 30 / 10

The classic decorating rule assumes a living room. A data-dense clinical table needs more neutral surface and less everything else — closer to seventy percent canvas and surface, twenty-five percent structural ink and borders, and accent rationed down to five percent: essentially one button, one link colour, one focus ring.

70% — canvas & surface 25% — ink & borders 5%

Never let decoration and meaning share a hue family

If a decorative accent and the "compatible / OK" severity colour sit in the same green family, a user's eye can no longer tell a stylistic choice from a clinical verdict. Look at these two side by side — one is decoration, one is a clinical result, and at a glance they're doing the same job on the retina even though they mean completely different things:

Just a themed button
Compatible
An actual clinical verdict

This is precisely why the locked severity tokens exist as their own set rather than "whichever green the active palette happens to use."

Gradients, used rarely and never on meaning

  • Fine on a large hero surface or a subtle background wash.
  • Never on body text, never on a small icon, and never anywhere near a severity badge — a gradient softens exactly the sharp edge a safety signal needs.

Section 06

Empty space

Whitespace is the one design tool that costs nothing to implement and is the easiest to get wrong under deadline pressure. A fixed scale, used with intent, is what separates "spacious" from "just under-filled."

The scale

4icon-to-label gap
8internal card gap
12related field group
16card-to-card gap
24component-to-component
32card padding, subsection gap
48section gap
64major section break
96hero / top-of-page breathing room

Micro versus macro

Anything under 16px is holding related things together. Anything 32px and up is telling the eye "this is a new idea." The most common real-world mistake is a value from the middle of the scale — 20 or 24px — used for both jobs at once, which reads as merely inconsistent rather than intentional in either direction.

The exception that matters most

A contre-indication banner keeps standard-or-greater spacing even in compact mode. Compact density is a convenience for browsing; it should never be allowed to compress the one kind of content where a rushed glance is exactly the failure mode to design against.

Section 08

Popups & overlays

Tooltips, toasts, modals, dropdowns — four different weights of interruption, and each should feel like a different amount of "stop what you're doing." Everything here is live.

Tooltip — hover or focus the icons

Lightest touch of the four — appears on hover with no click, dismisses itself, never blocks anything.

Toast — trigger one

Confirms something already happened. Auto-dismisses. Never demands a response — if it needs one, it should have been a modal.

Modal — open one

Blocks the page until answered. The destructive variant gets a top accent stripe in the locked danger colour and a red confirm button — never the platform's own accent colour, which would make deletion look like a normal, friendly action.

Dropdown / popover

Anchored to what opened it, closes on outside click or Escape. Note the danger item is separated by a divider and coloured — never placed loose among ordinary actions.

Backdrop treatment

Dim only
Blur only
Dim + blur — recommended

Dim alone can leave distracting colour showing through; blur alone can leave a modal feeling like it's floating with no separation. Together, at moderate strength, the background reads as "still there, clearly not now."

One more rule: stacking order

Tooltip above toast above modal above popover above a sticky header — in that order. A toast should stay visible even if it fires while a modal is open; a tooltip should never be trapped behind anything.

The rules worth remembering after you close this tab

  • Nine roles make a palette, not nine colours picked by eye.
  • Seven severity levels are locked, permanently, outside every palette.
  • One accent per screen — 70/25/5, not 60/30/10.
  • A border sits exactly between surface and ink, never at either end.
  • Four popup weights for four different amounts of interruption — tooltip, toast, modal, dropdown, in that order of how much they demand.
  • Compact density shrinks everything except a safety alert.
